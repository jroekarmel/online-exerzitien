Introduction

God is present: let us learn to remain with him.

Each year, Advent returns as a time when we prepare interiorly to celebrate Christmas, an unprecedented and decisive event for humanity: God became man among men. From his birth to his death on the Cross, the Son of God shared our human condition in every respect except sin. The days grow shorter, the nights grow longer, and the Church invites us to lift our eyes to the One who is coming. Each of us is called to watchfulness, conversion of heart, and renewal of life.

This waiting has several dimensions. We prepare to celebrate Christ’s birth in Bethlehem, while our hope also looks toward his return in glory. Yet a third coming takes place between these two, one that is more silent and happens every day: the Lord comes to meet us and knocks each day at the door of our hearts. Here lies the newness of the Gospel: even now, we can draw life from Jesus’ resurrection.

I. Brother Lawrence, Witness to the Presence of God

This Advent retreat seeks to lead us to recognize Christ’s daily coming. It draws on the teaching of a seventeenth-century Discalced Carmelite friar whose hidden life continues to enlighten many believers today: Brother Lawrence of the Resurrection.*

Brother Lawrence, whose baptismal name was Nicolas Herman, was born in 1614 in Hériménil, Lorraine, into a deeply Christian family.¹ At eighteen, he had a spiritual experience that left a deep impression on him. It was winter, and the days were short and gray. Work in the fields of Lorraine had stopped. The woods were damp and had lost their autumn colors, and the birds no longer sang. For Nicolas Herman, in the prime of youth, spring still seemed far away. As he gazed thoughtfully at a bare tree, time seemed to quicken its pace. In that rush of time, an unfathomable abyss suddenly opened before him. He grasped how the tree would soon be covered with buds and leaves and then bear fruit. Eighteen times already, he had lived through this yearly process of rebirth; but this time, the wonder opened onto a personal depth. Nicolas understood clearly that this whole phenomenon was not the work of the tree alone. He saw God’s power unfolding with order and purpose in this cosmic event. Year after year. Why did this process never come to a standstill? Nicolas was no longer contemplating a blind cosmos, but was, as it were, looking into the eyes of a Face. “He received a profound insight into God’s providence that has never been erased from his soul,” the account of his first conversation would later record (CN 1).² From that day on, God was for him “a living Reality whom he admired and to whom he could say, ‘I thank you.’”³

He then chose a military career and served in the army of the Duke of Lorraine during the Thirty Years’ War (1618–1648). Twice he faced death; finally, an injury forced him to leave military service at twenty-one. During his recovery, he recalled this spiritual experience and decided to devote his life to God. For a time, he lived as a hermit, but did not find the peace he hoped for. He then went to Paris, where he became a footman to a judge in the Parlement[the royal court of Paris]. At twenty-six, he entered the Discalced Carmelite monastery on the rue de Vaugirard in Paris as a lay brother and received the name Brother Lawrence of the Resurrection. Until his death on February 12, 1691, he led a humble life of prayer and work. After many years in the kitchen, he became a cobbler when his health no longer allowed him to carry out his earlier duties. Thus, he spent his life in Carmel doing ordinary tasks in service to the community. After years of interior turmoil, he received the grace of tasting peace of heart: his soul remained “fixed in God, above all things, as in its center and resting place” (SM 22). Before long, Brother Lawrence’s life was enlightened by daily attention to God’s presence, and its influence spread to those around him and beyond: “The holiest, most ordinary, and most necessary practice of the spiritual life is that of the presence of God. It is to take delight in and become accustomed to his divine company, speaking humbly and conversing lovingly with him all the time, at every moment, without rule or measure, especially in times of temptation, suffering, aridity, weariness, even infidelity and sin” (SM 6).

The writings of Brother Lawrence available to us come chiefly from two small books published by Father Joseph de Beaufort immediately after the friar’s death.⁴ The 1692 volume contained the Spiritual Maxims, the Letters, and the Eulogy of Brother Lawrence. The 1694 volume contained the four Conversations, The Practice of the Exercise of the Presence of God, and the Ways of Brother Lawrence. Father Joseph de Beaufort was vicar general to Bishop de Noailles, then bishop of Châlons-sur-Marne and, from 1695, archbishop of Paris. Having known Brother Lawrence for twenty-five years, Beaufort was his “anonymous” biographer and editor. We have sixteen Letters (L) written by Brother Lawrence during the last ten years of his life, addressed to various people. The Spiritual Maxims (SM) are a compilation of several of Brother Lawrence’s manuscripts. This short work centers on seeking God in daily life. Its authenticity is not in doubt, apart from the first of its eight chapters. We also have four Conversations (CN) between Brother Lawrence and Beaufort, held between August 3, 1666, and November 25, 1667. These were apparently written up thirty years later from notes taken at the time. A life of Brother Lawrence, called the Eulogy of Brother Lawrence (EL), is Beaufort’s first biographical sketch. Finally, two short works by Beaufort draw on Brother Lawrence’s writings to supplement the second volume, published in 1694. The Practice of the Exercise of the Presence of God summarizes Brother Lawrence’s entire thought. The Ways of Brother Lawrence is a second biographical sketch, focusing on Brother Lawrence’s sound piety and virtues. These works are valuable because they offer a brief portrait of the friar’s spiritual character by someone who knew him, although they contain little that is not already found in the sources mentioned above. No one disputes the authenticity of these sources. There is general agreement that Beaufort sought to transmit Brother Lawrence’s legacy as faithfully as possible.

Neither a renowned theologian nor a famous preacher, Brother Lawrence spent his days doing repetitive tasks, sometimes demanding and often silent. Yet in this ordinary life he made a discovery that may seem very simple, but has extraordinary spiritual depth: God is present; he is here at every moment, and we can live continually in his presence. This “living Reality” became the foundation of Brother Lawrence’s life and teaching. Writing about the practice of the presence of God, he explained: “This is, in my opinion, the essence of the spiritual life, and it seems to me that by practicing it properly you become spiritual in no time” (L 3).

This conviction, passed on chiefly through his letters, conversations, and the testimonies gathered after his death, has endured through the centuries. It continues to touch readers throughout the world today. More recently, Pope Leo XIV emphasized the continuing relevance of this teaching. In the preface he contributed in 2025 to a new Vatican edition of The Practice of the Presence of God, he explains that this work is among the texts that have most deeply shaped his own spiritual life and formed him on the path of knowing and loving the Lord. He presents Brother Lawrence’s path as both simple and demanding: to live constantly in God’s presence through small, continual acts of praise, prayer, supplication, and adoration, in every action and every thought. The Pope also emphasizes that as God’s presence becomes familiar and fills our inner space, the joy of remaining with him grows, while even daily tasks can become easier. Thus, Brother Lawrence’s witness is more than that of a seventeenth-century mystic: it offers a spiritual path for the men and women of our time.

II. Living in the Presence of God in Daily Life

Brother Lawrence’s message is deeply connected with that of the Advent season. Because waiting for the Lord means more than looking toward tomorrow; it means learning to recognize the One who is already here. The God for whom we hope is also the God who accompanies us, goes before us, walks beside us, and already dwells within us through the grace of his Spirit.

Our age is marked by constant agitation. The days follow one another at a steady pace, demands multiply, and we can easily feel that God no longer has a place in our schedules. Many dream of long periods of silence or solitude in which to pray more. Yet Brother Lawrence gently reminds us that encountering God does not depend primarily on external circumstances. It begins with an act of faith, continually renewed, that consists in simply turning our hearts toward the risen Lord Jesus.

So, this retreat won’t ask us to leave behind our family, professional, community, or pastoral responsibilities. On the contrary, it will invite us to fulfill them differently. Work, meetings, meals, travel, joys, and frustrations can gradually become privileged places where Christ lets us encounter him. Our path will follow the movement of the Advent liturgy and unfold around four major spiritual movements inspired by Brother Lawrence’s teaching.

III. A Spiritual Path for Advent

During the first week, we’ll discover that we can live in God’s presence at every moment. Even before speaking of conversion or spiritual effort, we need to open our eyes to a fundamental reality: God is already here. He always goes before us. Following the example of the sentinels stationed by the prophets on the city walls—“Upon your walls, O Jerusalem, I have posted sentinels” (Is 62:6; cf. Is 21:6–8; Hab 2:1)—we’ll be invited to cultivate the interior watchfulness to which Christ calls us: “Keep awake therefore” (Mt 24:42). We’ll do more than await the One who is coming; we’ll learn to recognize his presence already at work, his visits, which often pass unnoticed, and his silent calls in the midst of the most ordinary events of our lives.

The second week will help us discover that daily work becomes a place of encounter with the Risen One. Brother Lawrence encountered God in the midst of his tasks rather than by fleeing them. Amid the noise of the kitchen, the weariness of community life, and the repetition of the simplest actions, he learned to offer every action as an act of love. Following his example, we’ll seek ways to transform our daily responsibilities into a true liturgy of life.

The third week will take us further still: prayer is not limited to times of liturgical prayer, but embraces the whole day. Mental prayer is not reserved for a few special moments; it becomes a lasting disposition of the heart. Without diminishing the importance of the liturgy, the sacraments, or times of silent mental prayer, Brother Lawrence shows us how these moments nourish our whole lives, until they make every moment a dialogue with God. St. Paul’s words will then take on their full meaning: “pray without ceasing” (1 Th 5:17).

Finally, the fourth week will open onto the joy of Advent: Christian joy is born of this constant familiarity with God. As the soul learns to live under the gaze of the living God, the One who is and who remains with it, a deep peace gradually takes root within it (Ex 3:14; Rev 1:8). The soul then discovers that the God who revealed himself to Moses as “I am” is the God of Life, the Living One who never ceases to accompany us and call us to share his own life (Jn 11:25). This joy depends neither on success nor on favorable circumstances; it springs from the certainty that God remains with us. The Blessed Virgin Mary, together with Brother Lawrence, will guide us during these final days before Christmas. She is the first dwelling place of the Word made flesh, the one who welcomes the living God into her womb and, in faith, makes herself entirely available to his Word. At the Annunciation, she welcomes the One who is and who is to come, and her “yes” makes her the dwelling place where the Word takes flesh. Following her example, we too are called to become a dwelling place for God’s presence in the heart of the world.

So, this retreat will offer a true spiritual journey rather than a series of separate reflections. It will lead us from attentiveness to presence, from presence to offering, from offering to continual mental prayer, and from mental prayer to the joy of Emmanuel.

Each week will follow the same approach. Beginning with the liturgical readings for Advent, we’ll let the Word of God enlighten our lives. Brother Lawrence’s writings will then show us how this Word can be lived out in our daily lives. Suggestions for meditation, a proposed lectio divina, simple spiritual exercises, and a prayer will conclude each stage, so that reflection can gradually become experience. The aim of this retreat is therefore not to add another activity to an already full schedule. Rather, it seeks to unify our lives around a single reality: God’s loving presence.

At the end of this path, we may understand that Christmas is more than the anniversary of a past event. The birth of Christ Jesus in Bethlehem reveals God’s will to dwell among us. The Word became flesh to share our human condition and open us to his own Life. Contemplating the manger will therefore be more than the culmination of our Advent; it will become the path of our own hearts, called to welcome Jesus, the Living One. Brother Lawrence teaches us never to tire of returning to God, even after distractions, weariness, or falls. This humble perseverance is surely one of the most beautiful messages he can offer our age. Holiness is not reserved for a privileged few: it grows through these countless, trusting returns to the One who never ceases to stand at the door of our hearts and knock, humbly waiting for us to open to him (Rev 3:20).

May this retreat invite us to slow down interiorly, to listen more attentively, to recognize the visits of the Lord Jesus in the simplest realities, and to let this familiarity with God made flesh grow within us and gradually transform our whole lives. Then, when the angels’ proclamation rings out on Christmas night—“to you is born this day . . . a Savior” (Lk 2:11)—we’ll be able to welcome this Good News with hearts already prepared, not only because we have awaited him, but above all because, day after day, we have learned to live in the presence of the One who is Emmanuel, God-with-us, yesterday, today, and forever and ever.

Advent 2026 with Brother Lawrence of the Resurrection

Practicing the Presence of God

from November 25 to January 3 2027

Brother Lorenz of the Resurrection, a understated figure of the Carmelite Order, was one of the great masters of the spiritual life in the 17th century. His teachings, recently brought back into the spotlight by Pope Leo XIV, remain remarkably relevant today. This humble lay brother teaches us to place the presence of God at the center of our daily lives. His doctrine is rooted in the great Christian tradition and responds to Jesus’ call to “pray without ceasing” (Luke 18:1), in accordance with Saint Paul’s exhortation: “Pray without interruption” (1 Thessalonians 5:17). For Brother Lawrence, the way is simple: to remain at every moment in the presence of God, whatever our occupations may be. In this Advent season, we invite you to discover this school of the presence of God, so as to grow in a relationship that is simpler, more confident, and more intimate with the Lord.
